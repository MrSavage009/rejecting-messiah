# Why I Dissociate from Those Who Claim Islam but Spit on the Sunnah of Jesus

## A Critique of All Sects and a Call to Honor the Messiah

I write this as a Muslim who believes in the Quran as the final revelation of God. I write this as someone who affirms Muhammad as the Messenger of God. But I also write this as someone who has watched, with growing dismay, as people who call themselves Muslims—people who claim to follow the Quran—treat the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, as a closed chapter. They reduce him to a past figure, a prophet whose role expired, whose example is a curiosity rather than a model. They refuse to study the Gospel. They refuse to learn from the one whom the Quran itself calls al-Masih—the Messiah. And they call this Islam.

I have spent years studying the Quran. I have spent years studying the Gospels. I have spent years engaging with Muslims of every stripe—Sunni, Shia, Quranist, Sufi, Salafi. And I have come to a conclusion that I can no longer suppress: the partisans of every sect, each in its own way, are guilty of the deepest form of taqlid—blind following. They have inherited versions of Islam that exclude Jesus, that marginalize the Gospel, that treat the Messiah as a mere footnote to their own founders, their own scholars, their own Imams, their own shaykhs, their own interpreters. They have accepted these inherited versions without question, without investigation, without the slightest willingness to examine the sources that the Quran itself commands them to consider.

This writing is my response. It is my case. It is my dissociation.

I do not write this to divide. I write this to clarify. I do not write this to condemn individuals. I write this to critique systems. I do not write this as a Christian. I write this as a Muslim who believes in the Quran as the final revelation of God, who affirms Muhammad as the Messenger of God, and who also believes that the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, is alive, present, and returning. I write this because I can no longer remain silent while those who claim to follow the Quran ignore the one whom the Quran itself calls al-Masih.

## The Quranic Standard: What God Says About Jesus

Before I critique the sects, I must establish the standard. What does the Quran actually say about Jesus? What is his status? What is his role? What are we commanded to believe about him?

The Quran calls Jesus al-Masih—the Messiah—in numerous verses. In 3:45, the angels announce to Mary "the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary." In 4:171, he is called "the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary, the Messenger of Allah." In 5:75, he is again named al-Masih. This title is not a verb. It cannot be conjugated into past, present, and future tenses. It is a permanent designation. The Quran never revokes it. It never says his messiahship expired. It never says he was the Messiah only for a time and then ceased to be.

The Quran states that Jesus was not killed or crucified, but that Allah raised him to Himself (4:157–158). The classical understanding is that he was raised alive, body and soul, to the heavens. He is not dead. He is not a memory. He is a living person, awaiting the appointed time.

The Quran ties him to the Hour. In 43:61, we read: "And indeed, he will be a sign for knowledge of the Hour, so be not in doubt of it, and follow Me. This is a straight path." The dominant interpretation is that this refers to the descent of Jesus before the Day of Resurrection. His return is a major sign of the Hour.

The Quran says that the People of the Book will believe in him before his death (4:159). It presents him as a sign for the people and a mercy from God (19:21). It says God made him and his mother a sign for the worlds (21:91).

And the Quran commands us to consider the Gospel. In 5:46, it says that Jesus was given the Gospel, "containing guidance and light, and confirming what was revealed in the Torah—a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing." In 5:47, it commands: "Let the people of the Gospel judge by what Allah has revealed therein." The Gospel is not dismissed. It is praised. It is called guidance and light.

So the Quranic standard is clear: Jesus is al-Masih, alive, present, returning. The Gospel is guidance and light. We are commanded to consider it. Any sect that fails to honor this standard is deviating from the Quran.

## The Universal Flaw: Taqlid Across All Sects

Before I address each sect individually, I must name the common disease. The Arabic word taqlid means blind following—accepting a position because it is the position of your group, your school, your tradition, without independent investigation. The Quran condemns this. It commands us to think, to reflect, to consider, to use our reason. It commands us to follow the best of what is revealed, not the inherited opinions of our ancestors.

And yet every sect practices taqlid. Sunnis follow the four schools of jurisprudence. Shia follow the marjas and the Imams. Salafis follow their scholars and their literalist readings. Sufis follow their shaykhs. Quranists follow their own interpreters and their own rejection of tradition. Each claims to follow the Quran. Each interprets it through the lens of its own tradition. Each claims to reject blind following. Each engages in it.

This is the universal flaw. It is not limited to one group. It is the disease of the entire Muslim community. And it has a consequence: the marginalization of the Messiah. Because when you follow a tradition rather than the Quran, you inherit that tradition's blind spots. And every tradition has the same blind spot: Jesus. Every sect reduces him. Every sect neglects him. Every sect spits on his sunnah.

## The Sunni Flaw: Traditionalism Over Truth

Sunni Islam is the largest sect in the Muslim world. It claims to follow the Quran and the Sunnah of Muhammad, as recorded in the Hadith and the Sira. It has a rich tradition of scholarship, jurisprudence, and theology. But it has a fatal flaw: it has elevated the Sunnah of Muhammad to a position that often eclipses the Quran itself, and in doing so, it has marginalized the Messiah.

Sunni Muslims recite the shahada: "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah." This is true. But how often do they mention Jesus? How often do they call him al-Masih? How often do they reflect on the verses that present him as alive, present, and returning? In practice, Jesus is reduced to a prophet of the past, a precursor to Muhammad, a figure who will return one day but has no present relevance.

The Sunni doctrine of tahrif—the claim that the Bible has been corrupted—is used to dismiss the Gospel entirely. Yes, the Quran says that some people distort the words of God (2:75, 4:46, 5:13). But the Quran does not say the Gospel itself was corrupted. It commands Christians to judge by the Gospel (5:47). It calls the Gospel guidance and light (5:46). If the Gospel were hopelessly corrupted, why would the Quran command Christians to judge by it? Why would it praise it?

Sunni Muslims practice taqlid to the four schools of jurisprudence. They accept the rulings of their scholars without independent investigation. They claim to follow the Sunnah, but they ignore the Sunnah of Jesus. They honor Muhammad, but they do not honor the one whom Muhammad himself honored. The Prophet said, "I am the nearest of men to Isa ibn Maryam—no prophet arose between him and me; and he will descend." If Muhammad honored Jesus, why do his followers not do the same?

The Sunni flaw is not that they reject Jesus. It is that they reduce him. They make him a footnote to Muhammad. They treat the Gospel as a corrupted book with no value. They practice taqlid to their scholars while claiming to follow the Quran. And they spit on the sunnah of Jesus.

## The Shia Flaw: Imams Over the Messiah

Shia Islam shares many beliefs with Sunni Islam, but it adds a distinctive emphasis on the Imams—the descendants of the Prophet through his daughter Fatima and his cousin Ali. Shia Muslims believe that the Imams are divinely appointed leaders, infallible guides, and the rightful successors to Muhammad. This belief has profound implications for how they view Jesus.

Like Sunnis, Shia Muslims call Jesus a prophet and messenger. They honor him as al-Masih. But they also see him as subordinate to the Imams. In Shia theology, the Imams are the ultimate authorities, the ones who possess the esoteric knowledge of the Quran, the ones who intercede for believers. Jesus is a prophet, but the Imams are more than prophets—they are the chosen ones, the purified ones, the ones whose suffering redeems the community.

This emphasis on the Imams has led to a marginalization of Jesus. Shia Muslims may revere Jesus, but they do not study the Gospel. They do not follow his sunnah. They practice taqlid to their marjas—their religious authorities—who issue rulings on every aspect of life. They claim to follow the Quran, but they interpret it through the lens of Imami theology.

The Shia doctrine of tahrif is similar to the Sunni doctrine. They believe the Bible has been corrupted. They dismiss the Gospel as unreliable. They do not consider it a source of guidance. And yet the Quran commands us to consider it. The Quran praises it as guidance and light. The Quran commands Christians to judge by it.

Shia Muslims also reduce Jesus to a past or future figure. They believe he will return with the Mahdi, but they do not emphasize his present aliveness. They do not see him as a living sign of the Hour. They do not follow his ethical teachings as a model for their own lives. They focus on the tragedy of Karbala, on the suffering of Husayn, on the intercession of the Imams. Jesus is a background figure, a prophet among many, but not the central figure he is in the Quran.

The Shia flaw is not that they reject Jesus. It is that they subordinate him. They make him a servant of the Imams. They practice taqlid to their marjas while claiming to follow the Quran. And they, too, spit on the sunnah of Jesus.

## The Salafi Flaw: Literalism Without Love

Salafi Islam is a reformist movement that emerged in the modern era. It calls for a return to the "pure" Islam of the Salaf—the first three generations of Muslims. Salafis reject many of the innovations and traditional practices of Sunni and Shia Islam. They emphasize the Quran and the authentic Hadith. They are often characterized by their strict literalism, their rejection of interpretation, and their willingness to declare others apostates.

Salafis claim to follow the Sunnah of Muhammad more faithfully than anyone else. They dress like the Prophet, pray like the Prophet, eat like the Prophet. But they ignore the Sunnah of Jesus. They do not study the Gospel. They dismiss the Bible as corrupted. They reduce Jesus to a prophet who came, delivered a message, and left.

Salafis are often the most vocal in their rejection of the Gospel. They say the Bible has been changed, that it is unreliable, that it cannot be trusted. But the Quran does not say this. The Quran says the Gospel is guidance and light. The Quran commands Christians to judge by it. If Salafis were truly faithful to the Quran, they would study the Gospel. They would consider its teachings. They would honor the Messiah.

Salafis also practice taqlid—not to the four schools, but to their own scholars. They follow the rulings of Ibn Taymiyyah, Ibn Abdul Wahhab, and contemporary Salafi scholars without question. They claim to reject blind following, but they engage in it constantly. They claim to follow the Quran, but they interpret it through the lens of their own ideology.

The Salafi flaw is not that they reject Jesus. It is that they ignore him. They are so focused on the Sunnah of Muhammad that they have no room for the Sunnah of Jesus. They are so concerned with correct doctrine that they have no time for the Messiah. And they, too, spit on the sunnah of Jesus.

## The Sufi Flaw: Mysticism Without the Messiah

Sufi Islam is the mystical dimension of Islam. Sufis seek a direct, personal experience of God through prayer, meditation, music, and asceticism. They emphasize the inner dimension of the faith, the purification of the heart, and the journey toward union with the Divine. Sufis have produced some of the most beautiful poetry and profound spiritual insights in Islamic history.

But Sufis also have a flaw: they often neglect the historical Jesus in favor of an inner, symbolic Jesus. They may revere Jesus as a Sufi master, a model of asceticism and devotion. They may quote his sayings and tell stories about his miracles. But they do not follow his sunnah. They do not study the Gospel. They do not see him as al-Masih, alive, present, and returning.

Sufis practice taqlid to their shaykhs—their spiritual guides. They submit to their authority, believing that the shaykh has special insight into the inner meanings of the Quran. This can lead to a neglect of the Quran's clear commands. The Quran commands us to consider the Gospel. It commands us to honor the Messiah. Sufis often do not.

Sufis also tend to spiritualize Jesus's role. They see him as a symbol of the soul's journey, a metaphor for the inner light. But the Quran does not present Jesus as a metaphor. It presents him as a real, historical person, alive in the heavens, destined to return. Sufis may honor Jesus in their poetry, but they do not honor him in their practice.

The Sufi flaw is not that they reject Jesus. It is that they abstract him. They turn him into a symbol, a metaphor, a spiritual ideal. They practice taqlid to their shaykhs while claiming to follow the Quran. And they, too, spit on the sunnah of Jesus.

## The Quranist Flaw: Rejection Without Replacement

Quranists are a modern movement that rejects the authority of the Hadith and the Sunnah. They claim to follow the Quran alone. They argue that the Quran is complete, fully detailed, and sufficient for guidance. They reject the traditions of the scholars, the schools of jurisprudence, and the sectarian divisions. They pride themselves on their independence, their rationality, and their commitment to the Quran.

But Quranists have a fatal flaw: they reject the Hadith but then refuse to study the Gospel. They accept the Quran's references to the Injil, but they do not engage with the Injil itself. They accept that Jesus is al-Masih, but they do not learn what that title means from the scriptures that the Quran commands them to consider. They claim to follow the Quran alone, but they ignore the Quran's own commands to consider the Gospel.

Quranists practice taqlid—not to the traditional scholars, but to their own interpretations. They follow the teachings of their own leaders without question. They claim to reject blind following, but they engage in it constantly. They claim to follow the Quran, but they interpret it through the lens of their own ideology.

Quranists also reduce Jesus to a past or future figure. They deny his present aliveness. They deny his role as a sign of the Hour. They deny his return. They say he was only a prophet, that his messiahship was only for the Children of Israel, that it expired with his death. But the Quran does not say this. The Quran calls him al-Masih. It presents him as alive. It ties him to the Hour.

Quranists are also inconsistent. They reject the Hadith because they say it is unreliable, but they accept the Quran's references to the Gospel without actually reading it. They reject the traditions of the scholars, but they accept their own traditions. They claim to follow the Quran alone, but they do not follow the Quran's command to judge by the Gospel.

The Quranist flaw is not that they reject Jesus. It is that they ignore him. They are so focused on rejecting the Hadith that they have no room for the Gospel. They are so concerned with their own interpretations that they have no time for the Messiah. And they, too, spit on the sunnah of Jesus.

## The Common Failures Across All Sects

What do all these sects have in common? Despite their differences, they share a set of failures when it comes to the Messiah.

First, they all practice taqlid. Sunnis follow the four schools. Shia follow the marjas. Salafis follow their scholars. Sufis follow their shaykhs. Quranists follow their own interpreters. Each claims to follow the Quran, but each interprets it through the lens of its own tradition. Each claims to reject blind following, but each engages in it.

Second, they all neglect the Gospel. Sunnis, Shia, Salafis, and Sufis dismiss the Bible as corrupted. Quranists simply ignore it. None of them study the Gospel as the Quran commands. None of them consider its teachings. None of them honor it as guidance and light.

Third, they all reduce the Messiah. They make him a past figure, a future figure, or a symbolic figure. They deny his present aliveness. They deny his role as a sign of the Hour. They deny his return. They treat him as a footnote to Muhammad, a precursor to the Imams, a symbol of the inner light, or a prophet whose role expired.

Fourth, they all ignore the Quran's commands. The Quran commands us to believe in all the prophets. It commands us to honor the Messiah. It commands us to consider the Gospel. It presents Jesus as al-Masih, alive, present, and returning. None of the sects fully honor these commands.

Fifth, they all spit on the sunnah of Jesus. They do not follow his way of humility, mercy, prayer, and self-giving love. They do not study his teachings. They do not model their lives on his example. They honor their own founders and scholars, but they do not honor the one whom God Himself honored.

## The Concrete Divergence: Fiqh, Family, and Economics Against the Sunnah of Jesus

The abstraction of "honoring Jesus" becomes concrete when we examine the rulings that the four Sunni schools agree upon—the consensus (ijma') that no one disputes—and compare them to the Messiah. But here I must be careful. Not every point of divergence is a point of critique. Some practices that appear to diverge from Jesus's teachings are actually shared with the biblical tradition itself.

### Polygamy: A Shared Tradition, Not a Divergence

Polygamy is often raised as a point of tension between Islam and the teachings of Jesus. But this charge requires nuance. Jesus never explicitly forbade polygamy. The New Testament does not contain a clear prohibition. Many of the prophets of the Hebrew Bible practiced polygamy—Abraham, Jacob, David, Solomon. The biblical tradition regulated it rather than abolished it.

The Quran likewise regulates polygamy, limiting it to four wives and commanding justice among them (4:3). It says, "If you fear you cannot do justice, then only one" (4:3). And it says, "You will never be able to be just between wives, even if you try" (4:129)—a verse that many scholars read as a warning against polygamy except in narrow circumstances.

So polygamy is not the issue. The issue is how it is practiced. Solomon's polygamy led him astray—his foreign wives turned his heart to other gods (1 Kings 11:1–8). That is the bad way. The good way is when a man can maintain justice, provide for his wives and children, and not neglect his responsibilities. Islam permits this, and the Bible permits this. Jesus does not condemn it. So this is not a point of divergence between Jesus and Islam. It is a point of convergence.

### Slavery: A Bad Example

Slavery is also raised as a point of critique, but this too is a bad example. The Quran and the Sunnah regulate slavery and encourage freeing slaves. The Quran commands: "It is not for a believer to kill a believer... and whoever kills a believer by mistake must free a believing slave" (4:92). It commands: "If any of your slaves ask for a writ of emancipation, give it to them if you know any good in them" (24:33). It says: "Righteousness is... to free the slave" (90:13). The Prophet said: "Whoever frees a slave, Allah will free him from the Fire."

The Sunnah of Jesus also operated within a world where slavery existed. Jesus did not lead a slave revolt. He did not call for the abolition of slavery as a political institution. He healed the centurion's servant (Matthew 8:5–13). He told parables about slaves and masters. The early Christian community included both slaves and masters (Ephesians 6:5–9; Colossians 3:22–4:1). The abolition of slavery came centuries later through Christian conscience, but Jesus did not command it directly.

So slavery is not the issue either. The issue is the treatment of slaves—the way the Sunnah of Jesus and the Sunnah of Muhammad both command kindness, justice, and the encouragement of freedom. This is not a point of divergence. It is a point of convergence.

### Divorce: The Worst of Permissible Things

Divorce is where the divergence becomes real. All four Sunni schools agree that a husband may divorce his wife unilaterally, without cause, without her consent, without a court. He can say "I divorce you" and it is done. The wife has no such power. She must seek a judge or a khul' (redemption), which requires returning her dowry or obtaining her husband's consent.

The Prophet said: "The most hated of permissible things to Allah is divorce" (Abu Dawud 2178). So divorce is permitted but hated. It is the worst of halal things. It is not punished, but it is discouraged.

But here is the divergence: Jesus said, "What God has joined together, let no one separate" (Matthew 19:6). He permitted divorce only for sexual immorality (Matthew 5:32). He said that anyone who divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery (Luke 16:18). The Torah permitted divorce because of hardness of heart (Matthew 19:8), but Jesus called his followers to a higher standard.

Islamic fiqh permits divorce for any reason or none. It is the husband's unilateral right. It is the worst of halal things, but it is still halal. The Sunnah of Jesus calls for permanence, fidelity, and forgiveness. The four schools call for legal procedure. The difference is not between permission and prohibition. It is between the lowest permitted standard and the highest called-for standard.

### The Hudud: Complexity and Mercy

The hudud are often cited as a point of harshness and divergence from Jesus. But here again, nuance is required.

The Quran prescribes clear punishments for certain crimes: eighty lashes for slander (24:4), one hundred lashes for illicit sexual relations between unmarried persons (24:2), amputation for theft (5:38), and death for murder (2:178) or spreading corruption in the land (5:33).

But the application of these punishments is hedged by strict conditions. For zina, the Quran requires four witnesses (24:4). If four witnesses cannot be produced, the accuser is punished with eighty lashes for slander. This evidentiary bar is so high that conviction is nearly impossible in practice. The Prophet said: "Avoid the hudud as much as you can. If there is any doubt, let the punishment be avoided."

Stoning for adultery is not in the Quran. It is based on hadith and earlier scholarship. Some scholars, including the Quranist movement, reject it. But even those who accept it apply it only when the evidence is overwhelming—four witnesses to the actual act, or confession. This is not a system designed for harshness. It is a system designed to make punishment nearly impossible.

And here is where the story of Jesus and the woman caught in adultery (John 8:1–11) becomes relevant. The Pharisees brought a woman caught in adultery and asked Jesus whether she should be stoned. They were testing him, trying to trap him. Jesus said, "Let him who is without sin cast the first stone." They all left. Jesus said, "Neither do I condemn you. Go and sin no more."

The Islamic hudud are not designed to be the Pharisees' trap. They are designed to be a last resort. But the spirit of Jesus—mercy, forgiveness, restoration—is not absent from the Islamic tradition. It is found in the Prophet's own practice: he forgave the people of Mecca, he forgave the woman who tried to poison him, he forgave the man who urinated in the mosque. The hudud are for the worst cases. The Sunnah of Jesus is for the heart.

### The Prodigal Son: A Parable of Grace

In Luke 15:11–32, Jesus tells the parable of the prodigal son. A son takes his inheritance, squanders it on reckless living, and ends up feeding pigs. He returns home expecting to be made a servant. But his father runs to him—undignified for an elder—embraces him, robes him, puts a ring on his finger, and throws a feast. The elder brother protests: "I have served you all these years and never disobeyed. You never gave me a feast." The father says: "Son, you are always with me, and all that I have is yours. But we had to celebrate and be glad, because this brother of yours was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found."

Now imagine the prodigal under Islamic fiqh. He has spent his wealth on prostitution. Under the four schools, he is liable to the hadd punishment for zina: one hundred lashes if unmarried, stoning if married. There is no father who runs. There is a judge who sentences. There is no robe and ring and fatted calf. There is a legal procedure.

But here is the deeper point: the father in the parable represents God. Jesus reveals a God who runs to the sinner, who embraces him before he can even finish his confession, who restores him fully without requiring restitution. Islamic fiqh reveals a God who is just, who requires punishment, who cannot simply overlook sin. The two are not entirely opposed—the Quran calls God "the Most Merciful" (55:1–2) and "the Forgiving" (2:173). But the mechanism is different. In Christianity, grace is unmerited and transformative. In Islam, forgiveness is conditional on repentance (tawba), and certain sins require legal punishment.

The prodigal son is not just a parable about forgiveness. It is a revelation of the Father's heart. Jesus says the father saw the son while he was still far off and ran to him. He did not wait for the son to reach the door. He did not make him a servant. He restored him as a son. This is the sunnah of Jesus. This is the way of the Messiah.

## Economics: The Rich Young Ruler and the Camel

The economic teachings of Jesus are among the most radical in the biblical tradition. They are also among the most ignored—not just by Muslims, but by Christians too.

In Mark 10:17–27, a rich young ruler runs up to Jesus and asks, "What must I do to inherit eternal life?" Jesus recites the commandments. The man says he has kept them all. Jesus looks at him, loves him, and says: "One thing you lack. Go, sell everything you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. Then come, follow me." The man goes away sad, because he had great wealth. Jesus says to his disciples: "How hard it is for the rich to enter the kingdom of God! It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God."

Now compare this to Islamic economics. The Quran permits the accumulation of wealth. It says: "Allah has permitted trade and forbidden riba" (2:275). It commands zakat—the obligatory charity of 2.5% of one's wealth. It encourages sadaqah—voluntary charity. It praises those who give in charity. But it does not command the rich to sell everything and give to the poor. It does not say wealth is a barrier to salvation. It says wealth is a test (8:28), and that the rich must be generous.

The four schools developed sophisticated systems of finance, banking, investment, and trade. They permitted profit, rental, partnership, and the accumulation of capital. They prohibited riba (interest/usury) but allowed profit-sharing (mudarabah) and cost-plus financing (murabaha). They built an economic system that is not opposed to wealth, but regulates it.

Jesus drove the money-changers out of the temple (Matthew 21:12–13). He said: "Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy, and where thieves break in and steal. But store up for yourselves treasures in heaven" (Matthew 6:19–20). He said: "You cannot serve both God and money" (Matthew 6:24). He told the parable of the rich fool, who built bigger barns and said to himself, "Eat, drink, and be merry," but God said, "You fool! This very night your life will be demanded from you" (Luke 12:16–21).

The divergence is clear. Islamic fiqh permits and regulates wealth. Jesus calls for radical dispossession. Islamic economics builds systems for financial justice. Jesus says the rich cannot enter the kingdom unless they give everything away. The four schools developed zakat, murabaha, and Islamic banking. Jesus said, "Sell everything you have and give to the poor."

Now, this does not mean Muslims cannot be generous. The Quran praises the Ansar for giving half their wealth to the Muhajirun. It praises Abu Bakr for giving all his wealth. It praises Uthman for financing the army. The Prophet himself lived in poverty. But the system of fiqh developed by the four schools does not require this. It permits wealth. It regulates it. It does not call for its abolition.

And what about the poor? Jesus said: "When you give a banquet, invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind" (Luke 14:13). He said: "Whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me" (Matthew 25:40). He sat with tax collectors and sinners (Mark 2:15–17). He touched lepers (Mark 1:41). He welcomed children (Mark 10:13–16). He spoke with a Samaritan woman (John 4:7–26)—a woman, a Samaritan, a sinner.

The Quran commands kindness to the poor, the orphan, the captive (76:8–9; 90:14–16). It commands justice and mercy. But the intimate, table-fellowship of Jesus with the outcast is a distinctive mark of his sunnah. The four schools developed systems of charity, but they did not develop a culture of table fellowship with the marginalized. The sunnah of Jesus is to sit with the poor, to eat with them, to touch them, to welcome them as equals. The sunnah of the four schools is to give them zakat and keep them at arm's length.

## Family Dynamics: Patrilineal Authority vs. the Family of God

Jesus redefined the family. He said: "Whoever does the will of God is my brother and sister and mother" (Mark 3:35). He said: "If anyone comes to me and does not hate father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters—yes, even their own life—such a person cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:26). He said: "Call no one on earth your father, for you have one Father, and he is in heaven" (Matthew 23:9).

This is not a rejection of family. It is a relativization of family. The family of God takes precedence over the family of blood. The bonds of faith are stronger than the bonds of lineage.

Islamic fiqh, by contrast, is built on patrilineal lineage. The father is the head of the household. He has unilateral authority over his children's marriages. A mother's custody of her sons ends at seven (Hanafi) or puberty (others). The father retains control. Inheritance is patrilineal—sons receive twice the share of daughters (4:11). The family name passes through the male line.

This is not to say that Islam does not honor family. The Quran commands kindness to parents (17:23–24), maintains the rights of relatives (4:1), and praises the family as the foundation of society (30:21). But the structure of the family in Islamic fiqh is hierarchical and patrilineal. The structure of the family in the teachings of Jesus is radically egalitarian and centered on God as Father.

Jesus welcomed children as full members of the community (Mark 10:13–16). He healed the daughter of a Canaanite woman (Matthew 15:21–28). He raised the son of the widow of Nain (Luke 7:11–17). He honored his mother at the wedding of Cana (John 2:1–11). He entrusted his mother to the beloved disciple at the cross (John 19:26–27). He did not abolish the family. He transformed it.

The four schools preserved the family as a legal institution. Jesus transformed it into a spiritual community. The difference is not between family and no family. It is between a family defined by law and a family defined by love.

## Why I Dissociate

I dissociate from these sects. Not because I reject the Quran, but because they do. Not because I deny Muhammad, but because they deny the one Muhammad himself pointed to. Not because I am a Christian, but because the Quran commands me to honor the Messiah in ways that these sects refuse to do.

I am a Muslim. I believe in the Quran as the final revelation of God. I affirm Muhammad as the Messenger of God. But I also believe that Jesus is al-Masih. I believe he is alive. I believe he is present. I believe he will return. I believe the Gospel is guidance and light. I believe the Quran commands us to consider it. I believe the sunnah of Jesus is a treasure, not a threat.

I follow the Messiah. I study the Gospel. I seek to follow the sunnah of Jesus—his humility, his mercy, his prayer, his love for the outcast, his confrontation with hypocrisy, his self-giving love.
